---
title: How to make a study schedule you'll actually follow
description: A step-by-step way to plan your study week around real deadlines and real free time, with a simple weekly review that keeps the plan honest.
date: 2026-10-27
---

Most study schedules fail in the first week, not because the student is lazy, but because the plan was written for a person with more free time and fewer surprises. A schedule you'll actually follow starts from your real deadlines and your real hours, and leaves room for things to go wrong.

This takes about 30 minutes to set up and 15 minutes a week to keep going.

## 1. Put every deadline in one list

Before you plan any study time, you need to know what's due. Go through each course and write down every assignment, quiz, exam and project with its due date.

Check all the places due dates hide:

- Canvas (or your school's LMS): assignments, the calendar, and module pages
- The syllabus, especially for exams and big projects that aren't posted yet
- Announcements and emails from instructors
- Outside tools like Gradescope, WebAssign or a course website

One list, sorted by date. This is the part most people skip, and it's why they get blindsided by a quiz nobody posted on Canvas. (If your classes are on Canvas, here's [how to keep track of every Canvas assignment](/blog/keep-track-of-canvas-assignments).)

## 2. Find the hours you actually have

Take a week and block out everything that isn't study time: classes, labs, work shifts, commuting, practices, meals, sleep. What's left is your real study time.

Be honest. If you never get anything done after 10 p.m., don't count those hours. Most students find they have fewer free hours than they thought, and that's the point: you want to plan for the week you'll actually have.

## 3. Estimate each task, then add half again

For each item on your list, guess how long it will take. Then multiply by 1.5. People are consistently optimistic about how long work takes, and the buffer is what keeps one slow problem set from wrecking the rest of the week.

Break anything over about three hours into pieces. "Research paper" isn't a task. "Find five sources," "outline," "write the first two sections" are.

## 4. Work backwards from each deadline

Start with what's due soonest and place its pieces in your free hours before the due date. Then the next deadline, and so on.

A few rules make this work:

- **Finish a day early.** Plan to be done the day before anything is due, not the night of.
- **Spread big things out.** Studying for an exam across four or five shorter sessions beats one long cram session. Spaced practice is one of the most consistent findings in learning research.
- **Put hard work in your best hours.** If you think most clearly in the morning, that's where problem sets go. Save reading and review for when you're tired.

## 5. Make every block specific

"Study chemistry, 2–4 p.m." is easy to put off, because it's not clear what done looks like. "Chem 210: finish lab report sections 3 and 4" is much easier to start.

Keep blocks between 45 and 90 minutes with short breaks in between. Longer than that and focus drops off; shorter and you spend too much of each block getting started.

## 6. Leave empty space

Don't fill every free hour. Leave at least one open block a day, and a bigger one on the weekend. Things will run long, a new assignment will appear, a friend will need help. Empty space lets the schedule bend instead of break.

## 7. Track what really happened

A plan is a guess. To make better guesses, you need to know where your time actually went. Use a timer while you work and write down how long each block took.

After two or three weeks you'll see patterns: maybe problem sets take you twice as long as you plan, or you never study on Fridays. Plan around the person you are, not the one you wish you were.

## 8. Review every week

Once a week, Sunday evening works for most people, spend 15 minutes on this:

1. Add any new deadlines to your list.
2. Look at what you planned last week versus what you did.
3. Move anything unfinished into the coming week.
4. Lay out the next seven days using steps 4 to 6.

That review is what makes a schedule last past the first week.

## The short version

Collect every deadline. Find your real free hours. Overestimate. Work backwards. Make blocks specific. Leave room. Track your time. Review weekly.
